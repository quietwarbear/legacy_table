The keeper's handbook

Digitize the recipe cards — without losing the handwriting

The shoebox of stained index cards is one kitchen flood away from gone. Here's how to rescue every card in an afternoon — and why you must never retype-and-toss.

The handwriting is half the heirloom

Whose recipe is written in whose hand matters. The blue ink that went shaky in her last years, "good!!" in the margin, the grease spot exactly where a thumb held the card over the pot for thirty Thanksgivings — that's not clutter around the data. It is the data. So the rule for this whole project: digitize twice. Once as an image (the artifact), once as text (the usable recipe). Keep both, together, forever.

Step 1: Photograph like an archivist (10 seconds a card)

  • Daylight, no flash. Near a window, card flat, phone parallel above it. Flash bleaches ink and bounces off laminate.
  • Fill the frame, but leave a sliver of margin — cropped edges lose margin notes, and margin notes are gold.
  • Both sides, always. The back of the card is where "Doris's version — better" hides.
  • One card per photo. Batching four to a shot feels efficient and reads as regret later.
Fragile cards? Handle by the edges, don't flatten deep creases by force, and never tape a tear — archival photo first, repairs never.

Step 2: Transcribe — the fast way

Typing out a hundred cards is where these projects die. Let a machine do the reading: Legacy Table's recipe scanner takes the photo you just made and turns it into a structured recipe — ingredients, steps, timing — that you review and correct in seconds rather than retype. Faded pencil and cursive won't be perfect, and that's fine: fixing three words beats typing three hundred.

Attach the original photo to the digital recipe. Now the family gets both the usable version and grandma's handwriting every time they cook it.

Step 3: Capture what the card doesn't say

Cards are famously terse — "bake till done" from a woman who knew exactly what done meant. When you digitize a card from a cook who's still with us, spend one phone call filling the gaps, and record it. The card plus her voice explaining the card: that's the complete recipe.

Step 4: Store the originals kindly

After digitizing, the physical cards deserve better than the junk drawer: a photo-safe box, away from the stove's humidity, ideally with a note inside saying the collection has been digitized and where it lives — so nobody ever "helpfully" throws out the box thinking the recipes are safe elsewhere.

An afternoon, honestly

A hundred cards is about an hour of photographing and an evening of scan-and-review with your feet up. As a first project it's the perfect gateway: by the end you'll have the family's paper history safe — and a list of the cooks you still need to capture properly.
