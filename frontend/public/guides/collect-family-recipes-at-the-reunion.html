The keeper's handbook

Collect the recipes at the reunion — while everyone's at one table

Three hundred sixty-four days a year, your family's recipes are scattered across cities, kitchens, and memories. Then comes the reunion: one backyard, every keeper, every dish. Here's how to gather more of the family archive in one afternoon than in the whole rest of the year — without turning the cookout into a homework assignment.

The potluck table is the family cookbook

Look down the reunion spread and you're not looking at food. You're looking at an uncatalogued archive: Aunt Carol's macaroni that nobody else is permitted to bring, the tamales that only appear when the Ramírez side hosts, the rice your uncle guards like a state secret. Each dish has exactly one keeper — and this is the one day of the year they're all within thirty feet of each other.

Reunions are better for recipe-collecting than holidays, and it's not close. Nobody's trapped in a hot kitchen performing a dinner. People are relaxed, outside, bragging a little. And reunion dishes are a genre of their own — food made for folding tables and long afternoons, which means recipes that appear nowhere else and get written down least of all.

"We buried my great-aunt in March. At the repast, someone asked who had her punch recipe. Forty people looked at each other. Nobody did."

Before: claim the job quietly

1. Appoint yourself

Every family has a keeper — if you're reading this, it's you. Don't put "recipe preservation" on the reunion agenda or ask the group chat for permission. Projects create pressure; pressure makes elders wave you off. You're just the cousin walking around asking about food. That's all anyone needs to know.

2. Pick three dishes, not thirty

Before the day, name the three dishes your family would genuinely grieve. The ones with one keeper and no understudy. That's your whole mission. Anything else you capture is a bonus.

3. Charge your phone and bring a battery

Unglamorous, decisive. Your phone is the recorder, the camera, and the archive. A dead battery at 3pm has ended better plans than yours.

During: work the dessert table

4. Open with the plate, not the project

"Who made this?" is the whole technique. Then: "You have to tell me how you do this." Watch them light up. Nobody has ever been offended by being told their dish is the one worth writing down.

5. Get sixty seconds of voice, dish by dish

While they're telling you — and they will tell you — press record. You're not conducting an interview; you're holding a plate and being family. The judgment calls ("you want the oil talking, not shouting"), the history ("this was your great-grandmother's pan"), the corrections mid-story: that's the recipe, in the only format that keeps the cook inside it. (More on this in recording the recipe in their own voice.)

6. Photograph plates and hands

A picture of the finished dish settles a hundred future arguments about what it's supposed to look like. A picture of her hands crimping the crust is one your family will frame.

7. Take the roll call of missing recipes

Some keepers didn't come, and some dishes didn't get made this year. Ask the table: "What did Grandma June used to bring?" Write the names down — dish and keeper. That list is your assignment for the year ahead, and it's how the archive gets ahead of the next loss instead of behind it.

After: same week, while it's warm

8. Save it before the memory cools

Within the week, get each recording into one safe place. This is exactly what Legacy Table is for: play the voice note into the app and it writes the recipe — ingredients, steps, servings — while keeping the original recording attached to the dish forever. Share the family space with the cousins so the archive stops living on one person's phone. And when the collection is ready, it can become a printed cookbook where the pages play the voices — hand those out at next year's reunion and watch what happens.

9. Pass the plate

Before everyone scatters, recruit one cousin from another branch to be next year's collector. The table is long, and it shouldn't rest on one keeper — that's how recipes get lost in the first place.

This year's reunion only happens once

Whoever is at that table this summer will not all be at it forever — that's the quiet truth reunions carry. Three dishes, sixty seconds each, one afternoon of asking "who made this?" Your grandchildren will eat from the answers.
